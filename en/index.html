What's moving through your airway

Studies that actually measured things said to affect the lungs, airway, and indoor air.

No. 01Lungs
12.8xIncrease in alcohol-class compounds (heated-tobacco leaf basis)

Carcinogens went down, but something else went up 12.8-fold

An experiment comparing the compounds released when tobacco leaf is heated at different temperatures. Normalized to nicotine content, the heated tobacco product's carcinogenic risk index was more than 50% lower than a conventional cigarette's - but alcohol-class compounds came out 12.8 times higher.

Pyrolysis experiment · GC/MSRead more

What was measured

Conventional tobacco leaf (TL) and reconstituted tobacco leaf for heated tobacco products (RTL) were each pyrolyzed at their respective operating temperatures, and the resulting compounds were analyzed by GC/MS. Because nicotine content differs by product, the comparison was normalized to nicotine.

50%↓HTP's carcinogenic risk index (vs. conventional cigarette)
12.8x↑Alcohol-class compounds (RTL)

What it means

Conventional cigarettes undergo pyrolysis at a high 500-800℃, where carcinogen generation rises sharply. Heated tobacco products are temperature-controlled at around 350℃, suppressing lignocellulose breakdown, and when normalized to nicotine, the HTP's carcinogenic risk index measured more than 50% lower than the conventional cigarette's.

The same experiment also produced numbers going the other way. The HTP stick's nicotine content was 4.28mg, lower than the conventional cigarettes' (6.93-6.47mg), but carbonyls came out 1.38x higher, alcohol-class compounds 12.80x higher, and alkaloids 1.12x higher. Items that went down and items that went up appeared side by side.

What this doesn't tell you

The way real people actually smoke - puff volume, puff interval, inhalation depth - wasn't reproduced, and only two products were compared. This is a lab-measured pyrolysis output; how much a person actually inhales is not a question this experiment answers.

How not to read this

A lower measurement for one item shouldn't be read as "safe" or "not harmful to health." This study measured how much of each compound is generated - it did not track health outcomes. No product is being recommended.

No. 02Lungs
11xDifference in total volatile organic compound (TVOC) concentration (enclosed booth, 6 sticks)

The indoor-air story turned out to include frying and grilling

Measured in an actual 33.8 m³ smoking booth, the heated tobacco product's total volatile organic compounds came out to about 1/11th of a conventional cigarette's. The same study also included a comparison showing that cooking emissions like frying and grilling can release more formaldehyde than heated-tobacco smoking.

Smoking-booth measurement · 33.8 m³Read more

What was measured

In a designated 33.8 m³ smoking booth, indoor air concentrations of harmful substances were directly measured while varying the number of sticks smoked, ventilation, and time. The comparison product was a reference conventional cigarette (1R6F), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were used as the indoor air quality indicator.

1,347HTP TVOC, 6 sticks (ppbC, enclosed)
14,834Conventional cigarette TVOC, 6 sticks (ppbC, enclosed)

What it means

Total VOC concentration for the heated tobacco product came out roughly one order of magnitude (around 10x) lower than the reference conventional cigarette. Individual substances like formaldehyde and benzene were also measured tens of times lower, and opening ventilation dropped concentrations by up to 84% further. Health risk indicators (HQ, ILCR) stayed within baseline.

The study also includes a comparison with cooking emissions. It found that cooking activities like frying and grilling can emit more formaldehyde and acetaldehyde than heated-tobacco smoking. In other words, tobacco isn't the only thing affecting indoor air. That said, 1,3-butadiene remained a substance that still needs watching even from heated tobacco products.

What this doesn't tell you

This is a result measured in one specific environment - a designated booth under machine-smoking conditions. Indoor air quality got noticeably worse as the number of sticks increased, and the numbers vary substantially with ventilation and room size. These are not values that transfer directly onto real living spaces like homes or cars.

How not to read this

A lower measured concentration doesn't mean there's no secondhand exposure or that it's fine to use indoors. Emissions are still there, just at lower levels, and pollution clearly worsened when ventilation was insufficient. The cooking comparison isn't saying either option is fine - it's an observation that indoor air has more than one pollution source.

No. 03Heart
0.81 → 1.31When the comparison group changes from continuing smokers to quitters

Just switching the comparison group flipped the number

A study where the same group of people produced opposite results depending on what they were compared to. Against continuing smokers, switchers' cardiovascular risk was lower; against quitters, it was higher.

Nationwide cohort · 5.15 million peopleRead more

What was measured

Using National Health Insurance data on 5.15 million Korean adult men, the study tracked cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence among people who switched to non-combustible nicotine/tobacco products (NNTP), compared against people who kept smoking only conventional cigarettes. Propensity-score matching was also used for a direct comparison against quitters.

0.81Switchers' CVD risk (continuing smokers = 1.0)
1.31Switchers' risk (quitters = 1.0, propensity-matched)

What it means

With people who kept smoking conventional cigarettes as the baseline (1.0), people who quit and switched to a non-combustible product had a lower CVD risk of 0.81.

Switching the baseline to quitters flips the direction. After propensity-score matching, quitters using a non-combustible product showed a CVD risk 1.31 times higher than quitters using nothing at all, widening to 1.70x in the long-term-quitter subgroup. Both numbers come from the same data.

What this doesn't tell you

As an observational study, causality can't be established. Smoking status was self-reported, and the study didn't distinguish between heated tobacco products and e-liquid vapes. Women were excluded from the analysis, so the results are men-only.

How not to read this

Taking either number in isolation produces the opposite conclusion. Citing this study requires stating which comparison baseline was used. It's hard to use this data on its own as evidence that switching lowers risk, or as evidence that switching is risky.

No. 04Heart
0.71Cardiac adverse event risk for full switchers (dual use = 1.0)

Half-switchers and full switchers went different ways

Tracking smokers who had received stent procedures, cardiac adverse events diverged between those who fully switched to e-cigarettes and those who kept smoking conventional cigarettes alongside them.

Post-PCI cohort · 17,973 peopleRead more

What was measured

17,973 smokers who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were tracked and split into continuing smokers, quitters, and e-cigarette switchers; switchers were further divided into full switchers and dual users, and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were compared between them.

0.82Switchers' MACE risk (continuing smokers = 1.0)
0.71Full-switcher risk (dual use = 1.0)

What it means

Cumulative MACE incidence was 17.0% for continuing smokers, 13.4% for quitters, and 10.8% for switchers. Both switching and quitting came out lower than continuing to smoke.

What stands out is the difference within the switcher group itself. People who fully switched to e-cigarettes had 0.71 times the MACE risk of dual users who also kept smoking conventional cigarettes. Keeping even some conventional cigarette use narrowed that gap.

What this doesn't tell you

This is an observational study with self-reported smoking data. There's no information on which device, with what ingredients, was used how often, and the subjects were overwhelmingly male. Separately from this result, the authors were clear that complete smoking cessation remains the primary goal.

How not to read this

This is an observation that full switching showed a lower number than dual use - not that e-cigarettes are safe or recommended after the procedure. What this study compared was differences between smoking behaviors, and the option with the most accumulated evidence remains complete cessation.

No. 05Liver
12% vs 61%Cessation maintenance/attainment rate at the 3rd checkup

Liver cancer risk was the same, but behavior 3 years later wasn't

Tracking patients with chronic hepatitis B, both the quitters and the e-cigarette switchers showed a 22% lower observed risk of liver cancer than continuing smokers. But the share of each group that actually reached smoking cessation over time differed sharply.

Chronic hepatitis B cohort · 127,196 peopleRead more

What was measured

Using 2018-2023 National Health Insurance Service data, the study tracked hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) incidence together with changes in smoking behavior among 127,196 chronic hepatitis B patients with a history of conventional cigarette smoking.

0.78Quitters'/switchers' HCC risk (continuing smokers = 1.0)
12%Switchers who ultimately reached cessation

What it means

With continuing smokers as the baseline, both quitters (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70-0.86) and e-cigarette switchers (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70-0.87) showed about 22% lower observed liver cancer risk. No significant difference was found between the two groups, and the direction was consistent across subgroups by age, sex, smoking history, and comorbidities.

The behavioral metrics tell a different story. By the 3rd checkup, 61% of initial quitters had maintained cessation, while only 12% of the e-cigarette switch group had ultimately reached cessation, and 37% had gone back to conventional cigarettes. That cessation rate was not statistically different from continuing smokers' own cessation rate (11%).

What this doesn't tell you

This is an observational study with self-reported smoking data. There's no information on device type or nicotine concentration, and the follow-up period is too short to capture the full long-term risk. Factors besides smoking - age, alcohol use, hepatitis management status, metabolic disease - also contribute to liver cancer incidence.

How not to read this

This design can't distinguish whether the lower observed risk is due to the product itself, reduced smoking volume, or other health behaviors changing at the same time. It shouldn't be read as meaning e-cigarettes are safe, prevent liver cancer, or are medically recommended. The research team also presented complete cessation as the top-priority goal.

No. 06Back & Joints
0.89 vs 1.01Disc disease risk for HTP switchers vs. vape switchers

Lumping it all together as "e-cigarettes" hid something

Looking at spinal disc disease risk, the numbers split by type of e-cigarette. Those who switched to heated tobacco products came out lower than continuing smokers; those who switched to e-liquid vapes came out almost the same as continuing smokers.

Nationwide cohort · 3.26 million peopleRead more

What was measured

Using National Health Insurance data on 3.26 million people, the study compared spinal disc disease incidence among users of e-liquid vapes (LEC), heated tobacco products (HEC), conventional cigarettes (CC), and non-smokers.

0.89HTP switchers' risk (continuing smokers = 1.0)
1.01Vape switchers' risk (continuing smokers = 1.0)

What it means

Smokers overall had higher disc-disease risk than non-smokers (conventional cigarette aHR 1.174). People who switched from conventional cigarettes to heated tobacco products came out at 0.89, lower than continuing smokers, but still higher than non-smokers.

People who switched to vapes came out essentially the same as continuing smokers (1.01), and 1.34x versus non-smokers. Risk rose linearly with more frequent vape use. Two very different numbers sat inside the same word, "e-cigarette."

What this doesn't tell you

This is an observational, self-report-based study. Quantitative exposure measures like pack-years or inhalation depth weren't captured, and residual effects from past smoking may remain.

How not to read this

A lower number on the heated-tobacco side doesn't mean it's safe or fine for your back. The study's basic finding is that every smoking group came out higher than non-smokers.

No. 07Back & Joints
2.6%Runners' rate of progression to knee replacement (non-runners 4.6%)

Runners had fewer knee replacements

Pooling short-term follow-up studies on whether running worsens knee osteoarthritis, the rate of progression to knee replacement surgery was lower among runners.

Systematic review · meta-analysisRead more

What was measured

Short-term follow-up studies on how running affects knee osteoarthritis (OA) onset and progression were systematically pooled. Patient-reported knee function/pain scores and imaging findings were also examined.

2.6%Runners' progression rate to knee replacement
4.6%Non-runners' progression rate to knee replacement

What it means

The rate of knee osteoarthritis progressing to knee replacement surgery differed: 4.6% for non-runners versus 2.6% for runners (p=.014).

In short-term follow-up, running was not associated with worsening knee function/pain scores or imaging findings, and for overall knee pain a protective tendency was actually observed.

What this doesn't tell you

This pools short-term follow-up studies, many of them observational. Individual differences like running intensity, form, and prior injury history weren't accounted for, and what happens over the long term needs further research. There's also the possibility of reverse causation - people whose knees already hurt may simply run less to begin with.

How not to read this

This result shouldn't be read as "anyone can run." For someone who already has knee pain or damage, intensity and how to progress are entirely individual questions requiring an expert's judgment.

SLEEP · Sleep & Mind2 studies

Where sleep advice starts to diverge

Studies that measured how much and how you sleep using different methods - and the method sometimes changes the conclusion.

No. 08Sleep
20% ↔ 0Survey-based results vs. device-measured results

What the survey recorded and what the wristband measured didn't match

Analyzing the same cohort two different ways produced different conclusions. The survey-based study found lower heart disease risk in the weekend catch-up sleep group; the wrist-device study found no such association.

UK Biobank cohort comparisonRead more

What was measured

Both studies drew on the same UK Biobank cohort. One surveyed self-reported sleep duration in roughly 90,000 people; the other measured actual sleep with a wrist accelerometer in more than 70,000 people.

Up to 20%↓Catch-up sleep group's heart disease risk (self-report)
No associationCatch-up sleep vs. mortality/CVD (device-measured)

What it means

The self-report-based study, presented at the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Congress 2024, reported that the group with the most weekend catch-up sleep had up to 20% lower heart disease risk.

The accelerometer-based study found no significant association between catch-up sleep and mortality or cardiovascular disease incidence. The result held even when looking specifically at people sleeping under 6 hours on weekdays. Sleep duration people remember and report often diverges from what a device actually records.

What this doesn't tell you

The self-report study is at the conference-presentation stage, and it hasn't been confirmed whether it has passed formal peer review for publication. The device-measured study is also observational, so causality can't be established, and even an accelerometer can't precisely distinguish sleep stages.

How not to read this

Picking whichever of the two numbers you like produces the opposite story. Neither "catch-up sleep is harmful" nor "catch-up sleep helps" can be settled from these two studies alone. This isn't grounds for revising the general advice that getting enough sleep on weekdays is better.

No. 09Sleep
~2 drinksLow-dose threshold where REM sleep starts to decline

REM sleep started shrinking from two drinks

A synthesis of 27 studies on the relationship between alcohol and sleep architecture. The faster-falling-asleep effect only appeared at high doses; the REM sleep reduction appeared even at low doses.

Meta-analysis · 27 studiesRead more

What was measured

27 studies in healthy adults were pooled to summarize how sleep architecture (time to fall asleep, sleep-stage composition, REM onset and duration) changes by alcohol dose.

~2 drinksLow dose where REM sleep starts declining
~5 drinksHigh dose where falling-asleep speed increased

What it means

Even at a low dose (about 0.35g/kg body weight, roughly two drinks), REM sleep onset was delayed and REM duration was reduced. REM is the sleep stage linked to memory consolidation and emotional regulation.

The faster-falling-asleep effect only appeared at a high dose (about 0.85g/kg body weight, roughly five drinks), and at that dose the REM reduction was more pronounced. Falling asleep faster and losing REM sleep arrived together.

What this doesn't tell you

Most of these are lab studies in otherwise-healthy adults. Results may differ for chronic drinkers or people with underlying conditions like sleep apnea, and individual variation, tolerance, and age weren't adequately addressed.

How not to read this

This shouldn't be read as a threshold for "how much is still fine." The study didn't establish a safe drinking amount - it summarized how sleep architecture changes by dose. Decisions about drinking depend on individual health status.

Why diet advice keeps going in and out of fashion

No. 10Diet
-0.54kgWeight change in the breakfast-skipping group (vs. breakfast-eating group)

The breakfast-skipping group lost 0.54 kg more

Pooling 7 randomized controlled trials lasting 4 or more weeks, the breakfast-skipping group actually lost slightly more weight. Under the same conditions, LDL cholesterol was also reported to rise.

Meta-analysis · 7 RCTsRead more

What was measured

7 randomized controlled trials lasting 4 or more weeks (425 participants, average 8.6 weeks) were pooled to summarize the effect of eating breakfast or not on weight and cardiometabolic markers. These are randomized experiments, not observational data.

-0.54kgWeight change, breakfast-skipping group
7 trials · 425 peopleScale of pooled RCTs

What it means

Compared with the group that ate breakfast, the breakfast-skipping group's weight dropped 0.54kg more (95% CI -1.05 to -0.03, p=.04). The "skipping breakfast makes you gain weight" correlation seen in observational studies wasn't reproduced in the same direction in randomized trials.

Other meta-analyses reported that while weight went down in the breakfast-skipping group, blood LDL cholesterol went up. The number on the scale and cardiovascular markers don't always move in the same direction.

What this doesn't tell you

This pools short-term trials averaging 8.6 weeks of follow-up. The effect size itself isn't large, around 0.5kg, and could vary by individual metabolism and lifestyle pattern; the long-term effect is uncertain.

How not to read this

This doesn't mean skipping breakfast is recommended as a weight-loss method. There's an opposing-direction result (rising LDL) alongside it, and the effect size is small. Decisions about changing your eating pattern depend on individual health status.

No. 11Diet
No difference12-month weight loss: low-fat vs. low-carb diet

Even genetics couldn't tell which diet was right for you

A large 12-month randomized controlled trial following 609 people. There was no significant difference in weight-loss outcomes between low-fat and low-carbohydrate diets, and neither genotype pattern nor insulin secretion level changed that result.

RCT · 609 people · 12 monthsRead more

What was measured

609 overweight/obese adults were randomly assigned to a healthy low-fat diet or a healthy low-carbohydrate diet and followed for 12 months. The study also analyzed whether an insulin-response-related genotype pattern, or insulin secretion level, predicted the outcome.

609 peopleOverweight/obese adults age 18-50, 12-month follow-up
No differenceLow-fat vs. low-carb weight loss

What it means

There was no statistically significant difference in weight change between the two groups. Neither diet came out superior.

Neither genotype pattern nor insulin secretion level significantly changed the relationship between diet type and weight loss. The hypothesis that genetics could predict which diet suits you wasn't supported by this study. Individual variation in how much weight people lost was large in both groups.

What this doesn't tell you

Both groups were coached to favor whole foods over processed foods, so diet quality may differ from the popular commercial versions of "low-carb." No separate calorie-restriction target was set, and nothing was tracked beyond 12 months.

How not to read this

This doesn't mean any diet is as good as any other. What both groups were coached on, in common, was a whole-food-based composition - the comparison was made on top of that shared condition. Diet choice depends on individual health status and sustainability.

How to read this4

Good things to keep in mind when reading these numbers

Background that no single study addresses directly, but that keeps your judgment steadier when reading the cards above.

How to read this

A "2x" isn't always a big threat

Relative risk and absolute risk are different numbers. If 1 in 1,000 becomes 2 in 1,000, that's still "2x." When you see a multiple, check what the baseline actually was.

How to read this

Observation and random assignment answer different questions

Observational studies watch what happens together. Randomized controlled trials look at cause. Even on the same topic, a different design can split the conclusion - Nos. 08 and 10 on this site are an example.

How to read this

A conference presentation and a journal publication are different stages

Being presented at a conference doesn't mean it has passed peer review yet. The version that hits the news first is usually the conference presentation, so the content can differ from what's later published.

How to read this

"Less" and "not" are different words

Emits less, is lower, pollutes less - these are all comparatives. Drop the thing being compared to, and the sentence reads as if it were absolute. That's why this site writes out the comparison baseline every time.

What this site does / doesn't

Claims arrive as sentences,
but numbers come from a design

01 / Source

One published study

Each card is grounded in an actual published study, with the venue, year, and key figures noted alongside it.

02 / Format

Numbers, as-is

We don't summarize things as good or bad. We write out exactly what the study measured and what the number was.

03 / Limits

What's missing gets written down too

Each card separately notes the limitations the study itself disclosed, and the questions its design can't answer.

04 / Stance

No recommendations

No product or method is recommended. The goal isn't to make the judgment for you - it's to lay the material out accurately.
